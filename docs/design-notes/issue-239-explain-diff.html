Background

The card draws the whole solar system into a single SVG. Each planet gets an elliptical orbit ring, and next to each ring, where it crosses the vertical line that divides the seasons, two small labels like 1.0 AU — the top of the ring and the bottom. Those labels are the only place the card states a real astronomical distance in numbers.

A ring is built entirely from static data in src/astronomy/planet-data.ts: the semi-major axis au, the eccentricity, and longitudeOfPerihelion (which way the ellipse points). From those, the renderer computes the closest and farthest points of the orbit — perihelion au·(1−e) and aphelion au·(1+e) — and maps each from AU to a pixel radius with auToRadius(). That map is logarithmic: MIN_RADIUS = 40 px at the innermost orbit up to MAX_RADIUS = 360 px at the outermost, so Mercury isn't squished into the middle.

One more step sits between the data and the pixels. packOrbitRadii() sweeps outward from the Sun and, wherever a ring would collide with the one inside it, nudges it further out — a uniform radial push added to both the perihelion and aphelion pixel radii. Earth's push is the largest, because Earth's spacing bubble has to enclose the entire Moon orbit; and the push accumulates as the sweep moves outward, so it is worst at Mars. ellipseFromApsides() then turns the two (pushed) apsis radii into an ellipse, and orbitTransformComponents() rotates and places it so the Sun sits at one focus.

The label text was produced like this: verticalAxisIntersections() solved the two points where the drawn ellipse crosses the vertical axis, took each point's distance in pixels from the Sun, and ran that number backwards through the log scale with radiusFromAU() — the inverse of the AU→pixel map — to get an AU value.

What was wrong. The drawn ring is not at its natural radius. packOrbitRadii()'s anti-crowding push is baked into those pixels, and running the inverse scale over them turns the push into apparent distance. Earth's ring, shoved outward to clear the Moon orbit, reported 1.5 AU instead of 1.0. Mars, carrying Earth's push plus its own, reported about 3.0 AU — nearly double the truth. Mercury, Venus and Jupiter, whose natural radii already clear their neighbours and so get no push, read correctly. The ring positions, the planet markers and the Moon orbit were all fine; only the label text was wrong.

The idea

The ring is a pure function of static data. Nothing in the chain — AU to pixels, pack, place — takes the date. What moves from one frame to the next is the planet marker sliding along a fixed ring, never the ring itself. So the two label numbers for each planet are constants; they can be worked out once and reused forever.

And we already hold everything needed to work them out: au and e. The heliocentric distance anywhere on a Kepler orbit is

r = au · (1 − e · cos E)

where E is the eccentric anomaly — an angle that runs around the ellipse from perihelion. And verticalAxisIntersections() already computes exactly that: it parametrises the ellipse as (rx·cos t, ry·sin t) and solves for the t at each axis crossing. For that parametrisation t is the eccentric anomaly measured from perihelion. Feed it into au·(1−e·cos t) and the answer is in AU directly — the pixel scale never enters, so the packing push cannot leak in.

So: compute the two distances once, from the planet's natural (un-packed) ellipse, in a small dedicated module. renderOrbit() stops inverting pixels and simply places the two numbers it is handed, the smaller one next to the crossing nearer the Sun. That pairing is safe because a uniform outward push can't change which side of the orbit is closer to the Sun — screen distance stays monotonic in true distance.

Every corrected value lands inside the planet's true perihelion–aphelion band.

One honest caveat. auToRadius() is logarithmic, so the “natural” pixel ellipse is log-distorted, and its E at the axis crossing is not exactly the physical orbit's E for that screen direction. At the data's largest eccentricity — Mercury, e ≈ 0.21 — the discrepancy is far smaller than the 0.1 AU the label is rounded to; the bigger orbits are nearly circular and closer still. The label also sits at the axis crossing, not at true perihelion or aphelion, which the design note scopes as out of bounds for this change.

The code

Three source files, plus tests.

src/renderer/orbit-labels.ts — new, 38 lines

Builds ORBIT_LABEL_AU, a Record<planetName, { minAU, maxAU }>, once at module load. For each planet it shapes the natural ellipse from ellipseFromApsides(auToRadius(au·(1−e)), auToRadius(au·(1+e))), calls verticalAxisIntersections() on it, and takes min and max of au·(1 − e·cos E) at the two crossings. It passes a fixed eclipticViewDirection of -1: the crossing solve reads only the a, c, e components of the transform, none of which carry the ±1 ecliptic mirror, so the two eccentric anomalies — and therefore the table — are identical either way. The module depends only on PLANETS and svg-utils; it never imports orbit-packing, so the layout hack has no path back into the number.

src/renderer/bodies.ts

verticalAxisIntersections() is now exported (it was module-private) and each crossing it returns carries its eccentricAnomaly — the t it already computed. renderOrbit() gains a required fourth argument labelAU: { minAU, maxAU }. The old auAt helper and its radiusFromAU(Math.hypot(…)) inversion are deleted, along with the now-unused radiusFromAU import. Of the two crossings, the one nearer CENTER prints minAU, the other maxAU. Label placement — the x, y and offsets — is untouched.

src/renderer/index.ts

One import, and the orbit loop now passes ORBIT_LABEL_AU[PLANETS[i].name] as the fourth argument to renderOrbit(). Comet orbits are drawn by a separate function that has no labels and is untouched.

Tests

The red test in test/renderer/index.test.ts asserts every Earth and Mars label sits within that planet's real perihelion–aphelion range — it failed at 1.5 > 1.07 and 3.1 > 1.71. Ten renderOrbit cases in bodies.test.ts moved to the four-argument signature. A new orbit-labels.test.ts covers the table's structural contract; a new accuracy-orbit-distance.test.ts — following the repo's accuracy-* convention — pins every planet's distance against a golden physical table, checks each value against its real orbital range, renders the full scene to confirm the printed strings, and guards the #239 regression directly. The coarse render snapshot was regenerated; its diff is ten <text> label tokens and nothing else. Coverage stays at 100%.
